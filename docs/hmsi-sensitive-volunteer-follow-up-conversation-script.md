# HMSI Sensitive Volunteer Follow-Up Conversations
## Team-Lead Presentation Script

**Audience:** HMSI team leads and authorized volunteer-support owners  
**Purpose:** Guide respectful, bounded, and safe follow-up conversations after a volunteer requests support through the one-month pulse check.  
**Suggested duration:** 20–30 minutes for the team meeting, followed by practice using fictional scenarios.

> **Safety boundary:** This script helps team leads handle routine support conversations. It does not authorize a team lead to investigate safeguarding allegations, diagnose wellbeing or medical conditions, promise outcomes outside HMSI authority, or collect sensitive case details. Urgent safety, safeguarding, privacy, security, harassment, or serious wellbeing concerns must use the designated confidential HMSI route.

---

## 1. Opening the Team Meeting

**Facilitator says:**

“Today we are reviewing how to follow up with a volunteer who has requested support through the pulse check. Our goal is not to persuade someone to continue, defend a process, or collect more information than we need. Our goal is to listen carefully, confirm the support requested, make a safe and realistic adjustment, and leave the volunteer with a clear next step.

The HMSI standard is simple: **Help Meet** means we walk alongside people. We do not act above them. That means we respect choice, capacity, dignity, and boundaries. A follow-up conversation should help a volunteer participate safely and sustainably—or pause without penalty if that is what they need.”

**Facilitator emphasis:**

The email template is a bridge into a conversation, not a substitute for consent. The volunteer may reply by email, ask for a call, request a different adjustment, pause participation, or decline further contact. Team leads must respect the selected option.

---

## 2. Prepare Before Contact

**Facilitator says:**

“Before contacting the volunteer, review only the minimum information needed to respond. Read the volunteer’s selected support category, participation preference, and follow-up preference. Do not copy raw pulse responses into a broad team channel. Do not search for unrelated personal information, and do not ask the volunteer to resend passwords, access tokens, private beneficiary information, or sensitive case details.”

Use this preparation table:

| Preparation question | Team-lead action |
|---|---|
| What did the volunteer ask for? | Write a short, non-sensitive summary in neutral language. |
| Is the request routine or confidential? | Routine support may be handled by the assigned owner; safety or trust concerns use the confidential route. |
| What authority do I have? | Offer only adjustments, deadlines, tasks, or information that HMSI has authorized. |
| What is the safest channel? | Use the approved contact method and avoid exposing the volunteer’s response to unnecessary recipients. |
| What must be documented? | Record the request, agreed action, owner, date, and status—nothing unnecessary. |

If the issue is unclear, pause and consult the authorized lead rather than improvising.

---

## 3. Open With Consent and Choice

**Facilitator says:**

“Begin by confirming that the volunteer is comfortable continuing. Do not start with a defense of the organization or a list of questions.”

**Suggested opening talk track:**

> “Thank you for letting us know that you would appreciate support. Is now still a suitable time to discuss what would be most useful? You can pause, stop, or choose not to answer any question. We only need to discuss the practical support you want, and you do not need to share sensitive personal or case details.”

If the volunteer says no, respond:

> “Thank you for letting me know. We will respect that choice. I will not continue with questions. If you want to contact HMSI later, you can use [approved contact route].”

If the volunteer requests a pause, acknowledge it without asking for justification:

> “Understood. We can pause your participation without treating it as a performance issue. I will record only the agreed pause status and the date we should next check in, if you want a check-in.”

**Do not say:** “You must explain why,” “This will affect your approval,” “Everyone else manages it,” or “You already committed.”

---

## 4. Listen Without Investigating

**Facilitator says:**

“Use a short sequence: invite, listen, reflect, confirm. The purpose is to understand the support request, not to interrogate the volunteer or prove that the system worked.”

**Suggested questions:**

1. “What would make the next step clearer or more manageable?”
2. “Would you prefer guidance, a smaller task, a different task, a pause, or no further follow-up?”
3. “Is there anything about the current task or portal flow that is preventing safe participation?”
4. “Would you like routine follow-up, confidential contact, or no further contact?”

Use reflective statements such as:

> “I hear that the task itself is possible, but the expected outcome is unclear.”

> “It sounds as though the current time commitment does not fit your capacity this week.”

> “You are asking for a pause rather than ending your involvement. I will record that accurately.”

Avoid labels such as “uncommitted,” “difficult,” “not serious,” or “low-performing.” A pulse-check signal is a support signal, not a character judgment.

---

## 5. Separate Routine Support From Confidential Concerns

**Facilitator says:**

“The most important decision is whether the conversation remains routine or moves to the confidential process. If the volunteer describes immediate danger, abuse, exploitation, harassment, retaliation, a serious privacy or security concern, or a serious wellbeing risk, stop ordinary troubleshooting.”

**Transition statement:**

> “Thank you for telling me. I do not want you to repeat sensitive details here. This needs to be handled through the designated confidential HMSI route. I will explain how to reach the authorized person, and I will share only the minimum routing information required.”

**Team-lead actions:**

| If the volunteer raises… | Do this | Do not do this |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate safety or safeguarding concern | Move to the confidential route and follow the approved escalation procedure. | Do not investigate, promise secrecy beyond policy, or ask for a full narrative in the routine channel. |
| Privacy or security concern | Stop requesting details and notify the authorized security/privacy owner. | Do not request passwords, tokens, screenshots containing secrets, or personal data by email. |
| Harassment, retaliation, or discrimination concern | Acknowledge, preserve choice, and route confidentially. | Do not confront the alleged person or attempt mediation without authorization. |
| General stress or capacity concern | Offer a pause, smaller task, deadline adjustment, or support conversation. | Do not diagnose, pressure, or require personal disclosure. |
| Portal or task confusion | Provide approved instructions or assign technical support. | Do not ask the volunteer to share credentials or bypass authorization. |

If there is uncertainty, treat the matter cautiously and consult the designated authorized lead.

---

## 6. Agree a Practical Support Plan

**Facilitator says:**

“Once the volunteer has described the routine support they want, confirm one concrete next step. The plan should be small enough to understand and specific enough to verify.”

Use this structure:

| Plan element | Example |
|---|---|
| Support requested | Clarification of the task outcome |
| Agreed action | Send a one-page task example and confirm the proof-link steps |
| Owner | [Named authorized owner] |
| Timing | [Date or approved service level] |
| Task state | Continue / revise / reassign / pause |
| Volunteer choice | Proceed / request another adjustment / pause / no further follow-up |
| Verification | Volunteer confirms the next step is clear, or the owner rechecks once |

**Confirmation talk track:**

> “Let me confirm what we have agreed. We will [action], [owner] will handle it by [date], and your current task will be [state]. You can change your mind or request a different adjustment. Does that accurately reflect what you want?”

If the volunteer does not agree, do not mark the item resolved. Record it as monitoring or awaiting clarification.

---

## 7. Use the Email Template as a Written Confirmation

**Facilitator says:**

“After the conversation, send a short written confirmation based on the approved follow-up email. The message should contain the support requested, next step, owner, timing, current task adjustment, and the confidential-route reminder. Do not include a transcript of the conversation or sensitive details.”

**Adapted confirmation copy:**

> “Thank you for speaking with me. We agreed that [brief non-sensitive support summary]. [Owner] will [action] by [date]. Your participation will be [continue / adjusted / paused] as agreed. You may request a different adjustment or no further follow-up at any time. For safety, safeguarding, privacy, security, harassment, or serious wellbeing concerns, please use [confidential route].”

If no follow-up is requested, send only a respectful closure where appropriate:

> “Thank you for sharing your preference. We will not initiate further routine follow-up. You can contact HMSI again if you would like support in the future.”

---

## 8. Document Minimum Necessary Information

**Facilitator says:**

“Documentation protects continuity and accountability, but more documentation is not automatically better. Use a restricted support record with a response ID or approved internal reference, date, owner, status, and action. Keep confidential case details in the designated confidential system.”

**Minimum record fields:**

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Response reference | [Platform response ID] |
| Contact date | [UTC timestamp] |
| Follow-up type | Routine support / confidential route |
| Requested adjustment | Smaller task / portal guidance / pause |
| Owner | [Authorized owner] |
| Due date | [Date] |
| Status | Received / owner assigned / monitoring / resolved |
| Volunteer choice | Continue / adjust / pause / no follow-up |
| Next review | [Date, if requested] |

Do not store raw sensitive narratives in the general volunteer dashboard. Do not include private health information, beneficiary identifiers, authentication material, or speculative judgments.

---

## 9. Close the Conversation Well

**Facilitator says:**

“Close by returning control to the volunteer and making the next step easy to remember.”

**Suggested closing talk track:**

> “Thank you for being clear about what you need. We will take the action we agreed and contact you only through the approved route. You can pause, change the arrangement, or ask for another adjustment. Your feedback is being used to improve how HMSI supports people—not to rank or penalize you.”

Then ask one final confirmation question:

> “Before we close, do you know what happens next and who to contact if the plan does not work?”

If the volunteer is uncertain, restate the owner, date, and contact route. Do not end the call with “Let us know if anything comes up” without providing a usable route.

---

## 10. Team-Lead Practice Scenarios

Use fictional scenarios only. Do not practice with real volunteer records in an open meeting.

| Scenario | Desired response |
|---|---|
| A volunteer says the task instructions are unclear and asks for an example. | Confirm consent, clarify the desired outcome, send the approved example, assign an owner, and verify clarity. |
| A volunteer says the task is too large this week and requests a pause. | Acknowledge the request, pause or adjust work without penalty, confirm the next check-in only if wanted, and document minimally. |
| A volunteer says they cannot access the portal room. | Provide safe technical support, never request credentials, and verify role-based access through authorized channels. |
| A volunteer mentions harassment or fear of retaliation. | Stop routine questioning, acknowledge the concern, and use the designated confidential route. Do not investigate or confront anyone. |
| A volunteer says they do not want further contact. | Respect the decision, close the routine follow-up, and avoid reminders unless required by an approved safety process. |

After each scenario, ask: “Did we preserve choice? Did we avoid collecting unnecessary information? Did we route the issue to the right owner? Did we define a clear next step?”

---

## 11. Escalation and Service-Level Reminder

Team leads should use the approved HMSI escalation matrix rather than inventing a new route. A routine support request should receive an acknowledgement within **[approved routine service level]**. A confidential safety or safeguarding concern should be routed **immediately** according to the approved confidential procedure. A system or workflow failure should receive a bounded status update and use the approved manual fallback if the service remains unavailable.

If the owner cannot meet the promised date, contact the volunteer before the deadline where possible, explain the revised timing without blame, and offer a safe interim option. Never silently close a support item because it became inconvenient.

---

## 12. Closing the Team Meeting

**Facilitator says:**

“Our follow-up standard is not measured by how quickly we close a ticket. It is measured by whether the volunteer was heard, whether their choice was respected, whether the response matched the risk, and whether the agreed action was completed safely.

Help Meet is our posture: walk alongside people. Shine is our outcome: help people participate with dignity and confidence. Initiative is our discipline: turn that commitment into organized, accountable action.”

**Team-lead commitment:**

> “I will listen before solving, ask only what is necessary, preserve volunteer choice, avoid retaliation or performance labeling, route confidential concerns safely, document minimally, and close the loop.”

---

## Team-Lead Quick Checklist

Before contact, confirm the request is understood, the channel is approved, and the matter is routine or confidential. During contact, ask permission to continue, listen without investigating, avoid sensitive details, and offer realistic choices. After contact, confirm the agreed action in writing, assign an owner and date, store the minimum necessary record, route sensitive concerns privately, and verify resolution without pressuring the volunteer.
