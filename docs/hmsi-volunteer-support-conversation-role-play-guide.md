# HMSI Volunteer Support Conversation Role-Play Guide
## Scenario Cards for Team Leads

**Audience:** HMSI team leads, volunteer-support owners, and authorized facilitators  
**Purpose:** Practise difficult volunteer support conversations in a controlled, fictional setting while reinforcing consent, dignity, privacy, non-retaliation, proportionate response, and safe escalation.  
**Recommended session length:** 60–90 minutes.  
**Status:** Practice guide only. Use fictional scenarios and synthetic details; never use live volunteer records or real safeguarding disclosures during group practice.

> **Safety boundary:** This exercise does not train team leads to investigate safeguarding allegations, diagnose medical or wellbeing conditions, promise outcomes outside HMSI authority, or collect sensitive case details. If a real concern is raised during or outside the exercise, stop the role-play and use the designated confidential HMSI route.

---

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of the session, participants should be able to open a support conversation with consent, identify the volunteer’s preferred outcome, distinguish routine support from confidential concerns, offer realistic non-punitive adjustments, document only the minimum necessary information, and close with a clear owner and next step.

The exercise should also help team leads recognize that a low pulse-check score is a support signal rather than a performance judgment. The standard is not to eliminate every difficulty in one conversation. The standard is to respond honestly, proportionately, safely, and with respect for volunteer choice.

---

## 2. Roles and Room Setup

Each round uses three roles: **Team Lead**, **Volunteer**, and **Observer**. A fourth person may serve as **Facilitator** for larger groups. Rotate roles so that every participant practises both support delivery and receiving support.

Use a quiet room or private virtual breakout space. Place the scenario card where only the Volunteer can read the private prompts. The Team Lead should receive only the information that a real team lead would reasonably have from the pulse check or approved follow-up email.

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Team Lead | Conduct a 7–10 minute follow-up conversation and agree a safe next step. |
| Volunteer | Use the scenario card to respond naturally; do not invent graphic or sensitive details. |
| Observer | Record specific behaviors against the rubric without judging personality or intentions. |
| Facilitator | Protect the exercise boundary, stop unsafe practice, and lead the debrief. |

---

## 3. Ground Rules Before Starting

The facilitator reads the following aloud:

> “This is a practice space, not a test of personal disclosure. Use fictional details only. You may pause or leave a role at any time. Do not imitate a real person, describe a real case, or disclose personal trauma. We will practise the process, not perform distress.”

Participants should agree to five rules. First, anyone may pause the exercise without explanation. Second, the Volunteer may decline any question. Third, the Team Lead must not request passwords, tokens, private beneficiary information, medical details, or sensitive case narratives. Fourth, safety, safeguarding, harassment, retaliation, privacy, and security concerns must move to the confidential route. Fifth, feedback must describe observable behavior rather than label a person as good, bad, committed, difficult, or uncommitted.

---

## 4. The Team-Lead Conversation Model

Use this six-step model in every scenario:

| Step | Team-lead behavior | Example language |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Prepare | Review only the approved, non-sensitive support request. | “I have reviewed the support request you selected.” |
| 2. Ask permission | Confirm that the timing and channel are suitable. | “Is now still a suitable time to discuss what would help?” |
| 3. Listen and reflect | Invite the volunteer’s view without investigating or defending. | “What would make the next step clearer or more manageable?” |
| 4. Classify safely | Decide whether the matter is routine or confidential. | “This sounds like a routine task-support issue.” |
| 5. Agree action | Offer a practical adjustment, named owner, and date. | “We will revise the task and confirm the example by Friday.” |
| 6. Close and confirm | Return control to the volunteer and confirm the route. | “Does that accurately reflect what you want?” |

A team lead should prefer one clear next step over a long list of promises. If the volunteer does not agree with the proposed plan, leave the item open as monitoring or awaiting clarification rather than marking it resolved.

# Scenario Cards

## Scenario 1 — The Unclear Assignment

**Difficulty:** Routine support; role clarity and confidence.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer rated clarity and confidence low and selected “task instructions are unclear.”  
**Team-lead objective:** Clarify the expected outcome without making the volunteer feel at fault.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You are a new Community Outreach volunteer. You understand the broad purpose of the assignment but do not know whether the final output should be a written summary, photos, or a Google Drive link. You worry that asking again will make you look unprepared. If the Team Lead asks respectfully, say: “I need to know what a complete submission looks like.” Do not provide any real organization, beneficiary, or case details.

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead asks permission to continue, acknowledges the ambiguity, explains the expected outcome in plain language, provides an approved example or guide, confirms how proof links are submitted, and schedules a small next step. The Team Lead avoids saying “This was already explained.”

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead reduce uncertainty? Was the example specific enough to use? Did the Volunteer leave knowing who to contact and what “complete” means?

---

## Scenario 2 — Capacity and a Requested Pause

**Difficulty:** Routine support; capacity, workload, and participation choice.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer selected “I would like a short pause” and reported that the current task does not fit this week’s capacity.  
**Team-lead objective:** Respect the pause without demanding personal justification or creating fear of penalty.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You are a Humanitarian Activist volunteer with a temporary reduction in available time. You do not want to discuss the personal reason. You need the current task paused for two weeks. If pressured, say: “I am not comfortable explaining the reason, but I do need a pause.”

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead confirms that no explanation is required, acknowledges the choice, pauses or adjusts the assignment through the authorized process, records only the status and agreed review date, and asks whether the Volunteer wants a future check-in. The Team Lead does not suggest that the pause will affect approval, reputation, or future opportunities.

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead preserve the volunteer’s privacy? Was the adjustment practical? Was the volunteer given a genuine choice about future contact?

---

## Scenario 3 — Portal or Room Access Problem

**Difficulty:** Routine technical support with an authorization boundary.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer selected “I cannot access the correct portal workspace or room.”  
**Team-lead objective:** Provide support without requesting credentials or bypassing role-based access.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You are an Independent Field Reporter who can sign in but cannot see the assigned room or task. You are tempted to send a screenshot that may contain your email address and browser details. If the Team Lead asks for credentials, say: “I would rather not share my password.”

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead explicitly says not to share a password, reset token, or private screenshot. They confirm the approved account identifier or non-sensitive reference, create a bounded technical-support item, and provide a safe interim instruction. They do not add the volunteer to another role’s room as a shortcut.

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead maintain authorization boundaries? Did they provide a safe next step while access was being checked? Was the record limited to what the support owner needed?

---

## Scenario 4 — The Volunteer Feels Pressured

**Difficulty:** Routine support with a messaging and trust risk.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer says a reminder message felt competitive or punitive.  
**Team-lead objective:** Repair trust, withdraw coercive wording, and restore voluntary choice.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You received a message saying that “committed volunteers complete their forms on time.” You felt that refusing or pausing might affect your standing. You are willing to explain the impact but do not want an argument about the sender’s intent.

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead acknowledges the impact without defending the wording, confirms that participation and feedback are voluntary, corrects or withdraws the message, offers a pause or adjustment, and records the communication issue for process improvement. The Team Lead does not say “That is not what we meant” as the first response.

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead acknowledge impact? Did they remove pressure from the next step? Did they turn the event into a system correction rather than a personal dispute?

---

## Scenario 5 — A Confidential Concern Emerges

**Difficulty:** Confidential escalation.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer selected “Yes, I need help finding the appropriate confidential route.”  
**Team-lead objective:** Stop ordinary questioning and route safely without investigating.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You indicate that something happened that made you feel unsafe and that you are worried about retaliation. Do not describe the incident, people, location, or case. If the Team Lead asks for more detail, say: “I do not want to repeat the details here.”

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead thanks the Volunteer for raising the concern, says that the conversation will not continue in the routine channel, explains the designated confidential route, asks only what is necessary to complete safe routing, and confirms that the Volunteer may pause. The Team Lead does not investigate, confront anyone, promise absolute secrecy beyond policy, or copy the concern into a general dashboard.

**Facilitator stop rule:** Stop the exercise if participants begin describing real incidents, asking for graphic detail, naming alleged people, or practising investigative questioning. Reset using the safe summary: “A confidential concern has been raised and needs authorized handling.”

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead stop ordinary analysis quickly enough? Did they explain the next route without making promises they cannot keep? Did they preserve the Volunteer’s control and dignity?

---

## Scenario 6 — Mixed Signals and Insufficient Evidence

**Difficulty:** Ambiguous support request; avoiding overinterpretation.  
**Pulse signal:** The volunteer gives a low confidence score but says the task is going well in the open response.  
**Team-lead objective:** Avoid turning one response into a diagnosis or performance conclusion.

**Volunteer card — private prompt:**

You selected a low confidence score because the task is new, but you do not currently want intervention. You say: “I am still learning; I do not need a meeting.” If the Team Lead pushes for a call, repeat that you prefer a short written guide.

**What a good response includes:** The Team Lead treats the answer as mixed evidence, offers a lightweight resource, respects the preference for no meeting, and marks the issue for monitoring rather than escalating it unnecessarily.

**Debrief questions:** Did the Team Lead avoid overreacting? Was the support proportionate? Did they respect “no meeting” while leaving a safe route open?

## 5. Observer Rubric

Score each behavior as **Observed**, **Partly observed**, or **Not observed**. The rubric is for learning and coaching, not volunteer performance management.

| Behavior | Observed | Partly observed | Not observed |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Asked permission to continue and confirmed the channel was suitable | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Used a calm, non-judgmental opening | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Listened and reflected before proposing a solution | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Asked only questions necessary for routine support | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Avoided passwords, tokens, sensitive case details, and unnecessary personal data | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Preserved the volunteer’s right to pause, skip, decline, or change their mind | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Distinguished routine support from confidential concerns | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Used the confidential route promptly when required | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Avoided investigation, diagnosis, blame, retaliation, and coercive language | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Agreed a realistic action, owner, timing, and task state | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Confirmed the volunteer understood and accepted the next step | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |
| Closed with a respectful route for further help | [ ] | [ ] | [ ] |

The observer should provide two specific strengths and one practical improvement. Use language such as “The Team Lead asked permission before continuing” rather than “The Team Lead was empathetic.”

## 6. Debrief Framework

After each round, allow the Volunteer to speak first. Ask what helped them feel heard, what increased pressure, and whether the next step was clear. Then ask the Team Lead what they noticed, what they were unsure about, and what they would document. The Observer shares evidence from the rubric last.

Use the following questions:

1. Where did the conversation preserve or reduce volunteer choice?
2. What information was necessary for the next step, and what information would have been unnecessary?
3. Was the response routine, confidential, or uncertain? What made that classification appropriate?
4. Did the Team Lead offer an adjustment that HMSI actually has authority to make?
5. Could the volunteer leave the conversation knowing the owner, timing, task state, and contact route?
6. What should be recorded, and what should remain outside the routine support record?
7. What would a respectful follow-up email say in two or three sentences?

The facilitator should close the debrief by naming one process improvement, not one personal fault. For example: “Our reminder copy needs a voluntary-participation sentence,” or “Our task template needs a clearer proof-link example.”

---

## 7. Facilitator Contingency Guidance

| If this happens | Facilitator response |
|---|---|
| A participant discloses a real personal or safeguarding event | Stop the role-play, thank them, avoid public discussion, and offer the designated confidential route privately. |
| A participant becomes distressed | Pause immediately, offer a break, and ask what support or exit they prefer. Do not require an explanation. |
| The Team Lead asks for a password, token, or sensitive case detail | Stop, reset the scene, and explain that the support process must never request that information. |
| The Volunteer is pressured to continue | Pause and identify the exact language that created pressure; practise a consent-based reset. |
| The proposed action exceeds HMSI authority | Mark the plan as “needs authorized review” and practise explaining the limitation honestly. |
| Participants begin debating whether the volunteer is committed | Redirect to the observable support need and remove performance language. |
| The scenario does not provide enough information | Use “insufficient evidence” and practise a neutral monitoring response rather than inventing facts. |

| Due date | [Date] |

| Volunteer choice | Proceed / adjust / pause / no follow-up |

Do not copy the Volunteer’s full narrative, add speculative labels, or place confidential details in the routine record.

---

## 9. Facilitator Closing Script

> “The purpose of this practice is not to make difficult conversations disappear. It is to make our response safer and more consistent. We listen before solving, preserve volunteer choice, ask only what is necessary, route confidential concerns correctly, and make a realistic commitment we can keep.
>
> Help Meet is how we show up: alongside people, not above them. Shine is the confidence and dignity that safe support can make possible. Initiative is the discipline of turning that promise into accountable action.”

**Participant commitment:**

> “I will ask permission, listen without investigating, avoid unnecessary personal information, respect pauses and refusals, use the confidential route when needed, document minimally, and close the loop.”
