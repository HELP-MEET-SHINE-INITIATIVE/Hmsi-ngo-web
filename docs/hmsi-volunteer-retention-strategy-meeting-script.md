# HMSI Volunteer Retention Strategy
## Team Meeting Presentation Script

**Audience:** HMSI team leads, volunteer coordinators, programme leads, administrators, and authorized support owners  
**Purpose:** Turn one-month volunteer survey findings into a practical, respectful retention strategy  
**Suggested duration:** 35–45 minutes  
**Source:** HMSI One-Month Volunteer Feedback Survey and Survey Results Analysis Script

> **Meeting principle:** Retention is not about keeping people at any cost. It is about creating a volunteer experience that is clear, safe, respectful, purposeful, and sustainable for the people who choose to contribute.

---

## Meeting Outcomes

By the end of this meeting, the team should agree on the most important volunteer experience improvements, assign an owner to each action, define how progress will be measured, and schedule a follow-up review. The team should also identify any safeguarding, privacy, security, or serious wellbeing concern that must be handled outside ordinary retention planning.

The meeting should not produce invented survey statistics. Replace every placeholder with the actual response count, denominator, date range, and approved analysis before presenting the findings.

---

## Opening: Retention Begins with Respect

**Presenter script**

“Thank you for joining this review of our volunteer retention strategy. Today we are looking at the first-month experience and asking a practical question: **What would make it easier and more worthwhile for people to continue contributing to HMSI?**

We are not defining retention as simply keeping volunteers active. A volunteer may need to pause because of work, family, health, location, or changing availability. A respectful organization makes it possible to continue when participation is suitable, to reduce involvement when necessary, and to leave without pressure.

Our name gives us the right starting point. **Help Meet** means walking alongside people. **Shine** means helping contribution and potential become visible. **Initiative** means organizing support responsibly. A strong retention strategy must therefore listen, remove avoidable barriers, recognize contribution, protect wellbeing, and make the next step clear.”

**Facilitator note**

Set a non-defensive tone. Remind the team that feedback may describe system or leadership problems, not individual shortcomings. Do not name respondents or quote distinctive comments unless the person has explicitly agreed and the quote cannot expose them.

**Discussion prompts**

| Prompt | Purpose |
|---|---|
| What do respondents consistently value? | Preserve effective practices and avoid solving away what works. |
| Where do respondents experience friction? | Identify barriers in onboarding, role clarity, portal use, communication, or support. |
| Which findings are strong enough to act on now? | Separate clear action from issues requiring more evidence. |
| Which findings require follow-up rather than general discussion? | Route safeguarding, privacy, security, or individual support matters correctly. |

**Transition**

“We now move from what the survey says to what the experience may be asking us to improve.”

## Part 2: Diagnose Why Volunteers Continue or Disengage

**Presenter script**

“Retention is influenced by the day-to-day experience. People are more likely to continue when they understand the purpose of their role, can complete tasks without unnecessary friction, feel respected and safe, receive useful feedback, and can see how their contribution connects to community impact.

When people disengage, the cause may not be lack of commitment. They may be unsure what to do, unable to access the portal, waiting too long for a response, given tasks that do not match their capacity, concerned about privacy or safety, or uncertain whether their contribution matters.

Let us examine each theme without assigning blame. For every friction point, ask whether the cause is **clarity**, **capacity**, **connection**, **confidence**, or **safety**.”

| Retention lens | Diagnostic question | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Clarity | Do volunteers understand the role, task, approval boundary, and next step? | Simplify onboarding, task cards, role descriptions, and status labels. |
| Capacity | Do task expectations fit available time, skills, location, and equipment? | Offer realistic task sizes, flexible participation, and better expectation-setting. |
| Connection | Do volunteers know who supports them and how their work connects to HMSI’s mission? | Assign a contact, create regular check-ins, and show contribution pathways. |
| Confidence | Do volunteers know how to complete work and submit acceptable proof? | Add examples, supervised first tasks, feedback, and mentoring. |
| Safety | Do volunteers feel physically, emotionally, digitally, and socially safe? | Strengthen safeguarding, privacy, consent, escalation, and confidential reporting routes. |

## Part 3: Define the Retention Experience HMSI Wants

**Presenter script**

“Before selecting activities, we should define the experience we want every volunteer to have. A volunteer should be able to say five things after the first month:

1. **I understand why my contribution matters.**
2. **I know what I am authorized to do and where to ask for help.**
3. **I can complete my tasks through a clear and usable process.**
4. **I am treated with dignity and my information is protected.**
5. **I can see a realistic next step that fits my capacity.**

These are not slogans. They are design requirements for onboarding, assignments, portal features, communication, feedback, recognition, and support.”

**Team exercise**

Ask each lead to identify one current practice that supports these statements and one practice that undermines them. Capture the answers without naming individuals. Group similar issues into a short list for prioritization.

## Part 4: Build the Retention Strategy Around Five Levers

### Lever 1: Clear onboarding and role pathways

**Presenter script**

“Our first lever is clarity. New volunteers need a welcoming explanation of HMSI, a clear role pathway, understandable boundaries, and a named support contact. The first week should move from foundation to profile, workspace, safety, and a supervised first task.

The immediate action is **[action]**. The owner is **[owner]**. We will know it is working when **[measure, such as fewer role-clarification requests or stronger role-clarity responses in the next cohort]**.”

### Lever 2: Meaningful, manageable participation

**Presenter script**

“Our second lever is task fit. Volunteers are more likely to continue when they can see the purpose of an assignment and complete it within a realistic commitment. We should offer clear task sizes, priority information, due dates, required outcomes, and a safe way to report blockers.

The immediate action is **[action]**. The owner is **[owner]**. We will check **[measure, such as task acceptance, completion, blocker resolution, or continuation intent]**.”

### Lever 3: Support and connection

**Presenter script**

“Our third lever is connection. People should not have to guess who to contact or wait indefinitely for a response. A support rhythm might include an assigned contact, a short check-in after the first task, a clear room or channel for role-relevant questions, and a monthly opportunity to share learning.

The immediate action is **[action]**. The owner is **[owner]**. We will review **[measure, such as response time, support satisfaction, or unresolved questions]**.”

### Lever 4: Recognition and contribution visibility

**Presenter script**

“Our fourth lever is recognition. Recognition should be specific, truthful, and connected to contribution. It may be a timely thank-you, constructive feedback, a verified completion record, a learning opportunity, or an approved achievement badge. Recognition must not pressure volunteers to work beyond their capacity or expose private information.

The immediate action is **[action]**. The owner is **[owner]**. We will check **[measure, such as recognition coverage, volunteer-reported appreciation, or repeat participation]**.”

### Lever 5: Safety, privacy, and sustainable participation

**Presenter script**

“Our fifth lever is trust. Volunteers need to know that HMSI will take safety, safeguarding, privacy, consent, and security seriously. They must be able to pause, decline, report, or request support without retaliation or shame.

The immediate action is **[action]**. The owner is **[owner]**. We will verify **[measure, such as completion of safety orientation, awareness of reporting routes, or resolution of identified access concerns]**.”

## Part 5: Prioritize Actions Without Overpromising

**Presenter script**

“We will now prioritize actions using four questions: **How much does this affect safe participation? How many volunteers experience it? How feasible is the response? Can we measure whether it helped?**

Safety, safeguarding, privacy, and security matters are handled first through their proper confidential routes. For ordinary service improvements, we should select a small number of actions that can be completed and reviewed rather than create a long list that nobody owns.”

| Priority | Decision rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate referral | Safety, safeguarding, privacy, or security concern | Use the designated confidential route; exclude details from the general retention discussion. |
| Quick improvement | Clear, recurring friction with a straightforward fix | Rewrite an unclear task instruction or correct a broken support link. |
| Focused pilot | Important issue requiring testing | Pilot role-specific onboarding or a supervised first-task pathway. |
| Longer-term design | Structural change requiring resources or approval | Redesign volunteer matching, mentoring, or portal information architecture. |
| Monitor | Mixed, isolated, or low-confidence signal | Ask a focused question in the next survey or conduct a safe follow-up. |

**Facilitator instruction**

Limit the final meeting commitments to approximately three to five actions. Every action must have an owner, target date, verification measure, and communication plan.

---

## Part 6: Agree on Measures That Encourage Healthy Retention

**Presenter script**

“Measurement should help us learn, not turn volunteers into a target. We should track a balanced set of signals rather than a single activity count.

Useful measures may include first-month survey response rate, onboarding completion, role-clarity responses, task acceptance and completion, blocker resolution time, portal access issues, support response time, continuation preference, and the number of volunteers who choose to pause or leave with a respectful exit experience.

We should never interpret continued activity as proof that a volunteer is well supported. A volunteer may continue while experiencing stress, exclusion, or safety concerns. Qualitative comments and confidential routes remain important.”

| Measure | What it tells us | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| First-month response rate | Whether we are hearing from the cohort | Non-response may reflect access, timing, trust, or availability. |
| Role-clarity rating | Whether responsibilities and boundaries are understood | Averages can hide pathway-specific confusion. |
| Task completion | Whether assignments are feasible and supported | Do not treat completion as a volunteer’s overall value. |
| Blocker resolution time | Whether the organization responds reliably | Record only the data needed for service improvement. |
| Continuation preference | Whether volunteers see a suitable next step | This is an intention, not a guaranteed future action. |
| Support and inclusion feedback | Whether volunteers feel respected and connected | Protect small groups and distinctive comments. |

---

## Part 7: Create the Volunteer Follow-Up Message

**Presenter script**

“After this meeting, volunteers should hear what we learned and what we are doing. The message must be privacy-preserving and honest. We should not claim that every volunteer agreed or that every suggestion will be implemented.

The message should say what we heard, what we are changing, what remains under review, and how volunteers can raise a separate safeguarding, privacy, security, or wellbeing concern. Closing the feedback loop is part of retention because it shows that participation leads to learning and action.”

**Suggested follow-up language**

> Thank you to everyone who shared feedback about the first month with HMSI. We heard that volunteers value **[positive practice]** and would benefit from **[improvement area]**. In response, we will **[action]**, led by **[team or role]**, by **[date]**. We are still reviewing **[longer-term issue]** and will provide an update by **[date]**. Thank you for helping HMSI strengthen a volunteer experience built on partnership, dignity, accountability, inclusion, service, stewardship, and compassion.

## Closing Discussion

**Presenter script**

“Let us close by confirming the commitments. Which action will make the biggest difference to a new volunteer’s first month? Which action protects dignity, safety, or trust? Which action can we complete and test before the next cohort? Who owns the work, and when will we report back?

A strong retention strategy is not a campaign to persuade people to stay. It is a continuous practice of making participation clearer, safer, more meaningful, and more sustainable. We should make it easy for people to contribute, easy to ask for help, and safe to pause when their circumstances change.

That is how we live the HMSI name: we walk alongside people, help contribution and potential shine, and organize responsible support.”

## Follow-Up Calendar

| Timing | Follow-up |
|---|---|
| Within 3 working days | Send the privacy-preserving volunteer summary and confirm action owners. |
| Within 2 weeks | Review quick improvements and any unresolved access or support blockers. |
| Before the next cohort | Test revised onboarding, task instructions, portal guidance, and support routes. |
| At the next one-month review | Compare the new response set with prior results, using the same questions where possible. |
| Quarterly or as approved | Review broader volunteer participation, support, safety, and continuation patterns without unnecessary personal profiling. |
