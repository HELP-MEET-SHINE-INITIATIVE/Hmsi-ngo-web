## Executive Summary

HMSI’s next-quarter retention strategy should focus on improving the quality and sustainability of participation rather than treating continued activity as the only measure of success. Volunteers are more likely to remain engaged when they understand the mission and their role, can complete suitable work through a clear process, feel respected and safe, and can see a realistic next step that fits their capacity.

Based on the approved one-month feedback framework and quarterly dashboard design, the recommended priorities are **clearer onboarding and role pathways**, **faster operational support and feedback loops**, and **safeguarding, inclusion, and sustainable participation**. These priorities reinforce the meaning of HMSI: **Help Meet** means walking alongside people, **Shine** means helping potential and contribution become visible, and **Initiative** means organizing responsible action.

No actual quarter results were supplied for this memo. The dashboard should be populated with verified data before leadership interprets performance or sets numeric targets.

## The Three Strategic Priorities

### 1. Make the first month clear, guided, and role-specific

New volunteers should understand what HMSI does, what their selected role allows them to do, which actions require approval, who can support them, and what a suitable first task looks like. Confusion during the first month creates avoidable drop-off and can also create authorization, privacy, and quality risks.

**Next-quarter actions:**

- Refresh the welcome and onboarding sequence around the HMSI name, mission, values, role boundaries, and support contacts.
- Provide role-specific pathways for community outreach, community publishing, humanitarian activism, independent field reporting, campaign support, and digital advocacy.
- Add a supervised first-task pathway with clear task outcomes, priority, due date, expected proof, and a visible next step.
- Review portal labels and menus so each volunteer sees the workspace, rooms, opportunities, and task tools relevant to their role.

**Executive owner:** [Volunteer coordinator / programme lead]  
**Supporting owners:** [Portal owner], [role leads]  
**Success measures:** Onboarding completion rate, first-task activation rate, role-clarity survey signal, percentage of new volunteers receiving a named contact, and the number of avoidable access or role-boundary issues.

### 2. Turn support and feedback into a reliable operating loop

Retention depends on what happens after onboarding. Volunteers need timely answers, clear task instructions, help resolving blockers, and evidence that their feedback leads to improvement. Support should be designed as an operating process rather than an informal expectation that volunteers will know whom to ask.

**Next-quarter actions:**

- Establish a documented support route with an accountable owner and response expectation for portal, assignment, proof-link, and role questions.
- Review task cards for clarity, including required outcomes, time commitment, approved proof formats, and escalation instructions.
- Introduce a short check-in after a volunteer’s first task and record only the minimum information needed to resolve blockers.
- Review the retention dashboard monthly and send a privacy-preserving “what we heard and what we are changing” update to volunteers.
- Preserve truthful, specific recognition for contributions without creating pressure to work beyond a volunteer’s capacity.

**Executive owner:** [Operations lead / portal lead]  
**Supporting owners:** [Volunteer coordinator], [communications lead], [task owners]  
**Success measures:** Median blocker-resolution time, task completion rate, percentage of blockers receiving a documented response, support and inclusion survey signal, recognition coverage, and continuation intention.

### 3. Make safety, dignity, inclusion, and sustainable participation visible

Volunteers should be able to contribute, pause, reduce involvement, or raise a concern without pressure or retaliation. Safety, safeguarding, privacy, security, accessibility, and wellbeing must remain separate from ordinary retention scoring while still informing leadership decisions about trust and sustainability.

**Next-quarter actions:**

- Reconfirm safeguarding, consent, privacy, approved storage, secure-link, and escalation guidance during onboarding and task assignment.
- Ensure volunteers know how to report an urgent concern through the designated confidential route rather than through a general survey or public room.
- Review whether remote, field, and role-specific pathways have equitable access to information, equipment, support, and realistic task sizes.
- Provide an explicit pause or reduced-participation path that does not treat temporary unavailability as misconduct or failure.
- Review retention results only in sufficiently sized groups, suppressing small-cell or identifiable findings.

**Executive owner:** [Safeguarding / programme lead]  
**Supporting owners:** [Privacy or security owner], [Volunteer coordinator], [inclusion lead]  
**Success measures:** Safety-orientation completion, awareness of reporting routes, inclusion and dignity survey signals, documented resolution of access barriers, supported pause or exit availability, and confidential referral handling status.

## Measurement and Evidence Guardrails

Leadership should not approve targets until the baseline is populated from verified records. Each result should include a numerator, denominator, reporting period, metric definition, and context note. The dashboard should distinguish between operational signals, volunteer survey feedback, and confidential safeguarding, privacy, security, or wellbeing matters.

The retention rate should be defined before use. A recommended internal definition is:

> **Active-retention rate = active eligible volunteers at the end of the period ÷ eligible volunteers at the start of the period × 100.**

This measure should be accompanied by onboarding completion, first-task activation, task completion, blocker resolution, experience, inclusion, and continuation signals. A single rate cannot explain why people pause or leave, and continued activity is not proof that a volunteer is safe or well supported.

## Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Retention is treated as a target that pressures volunteers to stay | Track sustainable participation, supported pauses, wellbeing, and honest feedback alongside activity. |
| Small cohorts make individuals identifiable | Suppress or combine small groups and use anonymized themes. |
| Survey feedback is overgeneralized | Report the response count, denominator, collection period, and limitations. |
| Safeguarding or privacy concerns are discussed in ordinary reporting | Route them confidentially and keep them out of general retention scores. |
| Actions are agreed without ownership | Require an owner, due date, success measure, and volunteer communication plan. |
| Recognition becomes competitive or performative | Use specific, truthful, dignity-preserving acknowledgement tied to contribution. |

## Recommended Next Steps

Within the first two weeks of the quarter, leadership should confirm the three owners, approve the revised onboarding and support changes, and verify the confidential referral routes. By the first monthly review, the team should populate the dashboard with the baseline cohort and record any data-quality limitations. At the end of the quarter, leadership should review the action register, compare results with the defined baseline, and decide which changes to retain, expand, redesign, or defer.

The expected outcome is not merely a larger activity count. The expected outcome is a volunteer experience in which people understand how to contribute, receive practical support, feel respected and protected, and can choose a sustainable next step.
