# HMSI Volunteer Survey Results
## Presentation Script for Team Leads

**Audience:** HMSI team leads, programme coordinators, volunteer coordinators, administrators, and authorized reviewers  
**Purpose:** Guide a safe, consistent review of one-month volunteer feedback  
**Suggested duration:** 25–35 minutes  
**Source instrument:** HMSI One-Month Volunteer Feedback Survey Template

> **Facilitator principle:** We are reviewing the volunteer experience to improve how HMSI welcomes, supports, and works alongside people. We are not looking for positive answers, and we are not using the survey as a performance-scoring exercise.

---

## Opening: Why We Review the First Month

**Presenter script**

“Welcome, everyone. Today we will review the one-month volunteer feedback results and agree on practical improvements. The first month is an important point in the volunteer journey because people have had enough experience to identify what is clear, what is difficult, and what support they need next.

Our goal is not to defend the current process or search for someone to blame. Our goal is to listen carefully, identify patterns, protect volunteer privacy, and turn useful feedback into accountable action. This approach reflects the HMSI name: **Help Meet** means working alongside people, **Shine** means helping potential and contribution become visible, and **Initiative** means organizing responsible action.

By the end of this session, we should know what is working, what needs attention, which issues require immediate referral, who owns each improvement, and when we will check whether the change helped.”

**Facilitator emphasis**

Remind participants that survey responses should be handled only by authorized reviewers. The discussion must not reveal names, contact details, private comments, safeguarding information, or small-group identities unnecessarily.

---

## Part 1: Confirm the Evidence Before Interpreting It

**Presenter script**

“We begin by understanding the response set. Before discussing whether a score is high or low, we need to know how many volunteers were invited, how many responded, when the survey was open, and whether there were any collection or access problems.

Please record the invited population as **[N invited]**, the number of completed responses as **[N completed]**, and the response rate as **[response rate]**. We should also note whether responses came from different volunteer pathways, such as community outreach, publishing, activism, reporting, campaign support, or digital advocacy.

A response rate is context, not a verdict. A low response rate may mean the survey was difficult to access, poorly timed, or not trusted. A high response rate does not automatically mean the experience was good. We should interpret the results alongside the method used to collect them and the experiences that may not be represented.”

**Analysis prompts**

| Check | Team-lead question |
|---|---|
| Coverage | Who was invited, and who may have been missed? |
| Timing | Was the survey sent near the end of the first month as intended? |
| Access | Could volunteers complete it on mobile and through the channels they actually use? |
| Duplication | Are duplicate or incomplete responses being counted consistently? |
| Context | Did any onboarding, portal, assignment, or programme change affect this month’s results? |

**Transition**

“Now that we understand the evidence base, we can examine the main experience signals without overinterpreting a single number.”

---

## Part 2: Read the Ratings as Patterns, Not Rankings

**Presenter script**

“The survey uses a five-point agreement scale, with an option for not applicable. We should report the number of responses behind each result and keep the wording of the question visible. A mean score can be useful for a quick summary, but it should not replace the distribution of responses.

For each section, look at the proportion of responses that are positive, neutral, negative, or not applicable. A useful internal summary is the percentage selecting 4 or 5, the percentage selecting 1 or 2, and the number selecting N/A. We should avoid presenting these percentages without their denominator.

For example, say: ‘**[x] of [n] respondents selected agree or strongly agree that they understood the task workflow.’** Do not say: ‘Volunteers understand the workflow’ unless the evidence supports that broader statement.

Look for repeated signals across related questions. If onboarding clarity, role clarity, and portal navigation all score lower, that suggests a connected experience problem. If one item is lower but the open comments are positive, we should investigate rather than immediately redesign the entire process.”

**Recommended result table**

| Survey area | Positive responses, 4–5 | Negative responses, 1–2 | N/A count | What we need to learn |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Onboarding and welcome | [x%] of [n] | [x%] of [n] | [n] | Was the first welcome clear and paced well? |
| Role clarity | [x%] of [n] | [x%] of [n] | [n] | Do volunteers understand responsibilities and approval boundaries? |
| Portal and tasks | [x%] of [n] | [x%] of [n] | [n] | Can volunteers reach the right workspace and complete tasks? |
| Safety and dignity | [x%] of [n] | [x%] of [n] | [n] | Do volunteers understand consent, privacy, and reporting routes? |
| Support and inclusion | [x%] of [n] | [x%] of [n] | [n] | Do volunteers feel respected, included, and supported? |

## Part 3: Compare Experience Segments Carefully

**Presenter script**

“Next, we consider whether different volunteer pathways are experiencing the process differently. Segmentation can help us find a specific improvement opportunity, but it can also create privacy risks or misleading comparisons.

Only compare groups when there are enough responses to make the result reasonably interpretable. As an internal privacy safeguard, suppress or combine very small groups rather than reporting a result that could identify an individual. Do not publish a named volunteer’s response or quote a distinctive comment without permission.

Compare only factors that are relevant to service improvement, such as volunteer pathway, remote versus field participation, or portal use. Avoid unnecessary analysis of sensitive personal characteristics. Never infer motivation, competence, safety, or personal circumstances from a survey score alone.

If one group reports lower portal usability, ask whether its tasks require different features. If field volunteers report a different support experience from remote volunteers, ask whether the communication cadence or equipment needs differ. The purpose of segmentation is better support, not ranking volunteers or teams.”

**Segment review table**

| Segment | Minimum useful context | Safe question |
|---|---|---|
| Volunteer pathway | What activity the person selected | Does this pathway need different onboarding or examples? |
| Participation mode | Remote, field, or mixed | Are the communication and support routes equally usable? |
| Portal exposure | Used portal features or not yet started | Which screen or workflow needs clearer guidance? |
| Month or cohort | Collection period and onboarding version | Did a process change improve the experience? |

**Transition**

“Numbers tell us where to look. Open comments help us understand why people responded that way.”

---

## Part 4: Code Open Comments Without Losing Their Meaning

**Presenter script**

“When we review written feedback, we should code comments into practical themes while keeping the original meaning intact. Suggested themes include onboarding clarity, role boundaries, portal access, task instructions, proof submission, room communication, support and mentoring, inclusion, safeguarding, and continuation interest.

A comment may belong to more than one theme. We should record the number of comments in each theme, but we must not imply that the number of comments equals the number of volunteers unless each response was counted exactly once for that theme.

Use representative, anonymized paraphrases in the team discussion. Do not copy a distinctive sentence into a slide if someone could recognize the author. Keep sensitive or potentially reportable content out of ordinary thematic analysis and refer it through the appropriate confidential route.

We should also separate the type of feedback. A suggestion is not the same as a complaint. A reported access failure is not the same as a preference. A safeguarding disclosure is not a product-improvement comment. Each type requires a different response.”

**Comment coding framework**

| Comment type | Example handling | Owner or route |
|---|---|---|
| Suggestion | Add to improvement backlog and assess feasibility | Programme or portal owner |
| Confusion | Clarify instructions, labels, or onboarding examples | Volunteer coordinator or product owner |
| Technical failure | Reproduce safely and log the route, time, and impact | Portal administrator or technical owner |
| Exclusion concern | Review accessibility, inclusion, and communication practice | Team lead and inclusion owner |
| Safeguarding or privacy concern | Stop general analysis and refer confidentially | Designated safeguarding/security route |
| Positive practice | Preserve and share the practice without exposing identity | Relevant team lead |

---

## Part 5: Identify the Highest-Value Improvements

**Presenter script**

“We now convert findings into priorities. Not every issue needs a large project, and not every popular suggestion should be implemented immediately. We should prioritize issues that affect safety, access, role clarity, task completion, inclusion, or trust.

For each proposed improvement, describe the evidence, the affected experience, the action, the owner, the target date, and the measure we will use to check progress. A good action is specific enough that another team lead can tell whether it happened.

For example, instead of writing ‘Improve the portal,’ write ‘Rewrite the first task-card instructions, add an example of an acceptable Drive proof link, and test the revised flow with three new volunteers before the next cohort.’ The second action has a defined owner, deliverable, and verification step.

Separate immediate fixes from longer-term improvements. A broken link, unclear contact route, or incorrect role label may be resolved quickly. A change to volunteer training, room structure, or assignment design may require planning and approval.”

**Priority matrix**

| Priority | Use when | Example response |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate referral | Safety, safeguarding, privacy, or security risk is identified | Move to the confidential reporting route; do not debate details in the survey meeting. |
| High | A problem blocks participation or creates repeated authorization or access failures | Assign an owner and corrective action this week. |
| Medium | A recurring friction point reduces clarity or confidence but does not block work | Add to the next onboarding or portal improvement cycle. |
| Monitor | A mixed or isolated signal needs more evidence | Collect targeted follow-up or observe the next cohort. |
| Preserve | A practice is consistently helpful and aligned with HMSI values | Document it and make it part of the standard onboarding experience. |

---

## Part 6: Connect Findings to HMSI Brand Values

**Presenter script**

“The survey is also a check on whether our values are visible in the volunteer experience.

If volunteers say they can raise concerns honestly, that supports **integrity** and **accountability**. If they feel their time, privacy, and contribution are respected, that supports **dignity** and **stewardship**. If the portal and communication routes are understandable and usable, that supports **inclusion**. If roles are coordinated without unnecessary hierarchy, that supports **partnership**. If volunteers can see how their work contributes to community outcomes, that supports **service** and **compassion**.

We should not use values as decorative labels. We should connect each value to a behaviour and an improvement. For example, ‘partnership’ may mean involving volunteers in testing a new task card. ‘Dignity’ may mean removing a public-facing request for unnecessary personal information. ‘Initiative’ may mean assigning an owner and date to a known onboarding issue.”

**Values-to-action table**

| HMSI value | Feedback signal | Practical leadership response |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity | Volunteers are unsure what is verified or official | Clarify status labels and approval boundaries. |
| Dignity | Volunteers feel exposed, pressured, or poorly represented | Review consent, privacy, and communication practice. |
| Accountability | Blockers remain unresolved or owners are unclear | Publish an action owner and follow-up date. |
| Inclusion | Some volunteers cannot use a route or understand instructions | Improve accessibility, mobile usability, and plain language. |
| Partnership | Volunteers feel decisions are made without listening | Include volunteer input in process testing and review. |
| Stewardship | Sensitive information or credentials are handled unsafely | Reinforce minimum-necessary access and secure channels. |

---

## Part 7: Close the Feedback Loop

**Presenter script**

“Feedback becomes meaningful when volunteers can see what happened after they responded. We should publish or send a short, privacy-preserving summary that says what we heard, what we are changing, what we are still reviewing, and how volunteers can raise a concern outside the survey.

Do not promise that every suggestion will be adopted. Explain the decision honestly. If an issue cannot be changed immediately, say why and identify the next review point. If a change is made, tell people how to use it.

The follow-up should preserve anonymity. We can say that several respondents reported difficulty understanding proof-link requirements without naming anyone or quoting a distinctive comment. We can then state the action: revise the guidance, add an example, and test it with the next cohort.

Close the loop with the people who contributed. This is how HMSI demonstrates that Help Meet is not only a phrase; it is a practice of listening and working alongside people.”

**Close-the-loop template**

> **What we heard:** Volunteers asked for clearer task outcomes and proof-link examples.  
> **What we are changing:** HMSI will revise the task-card guidance and add a safe example of an approved Drive link.  
> **What we are still reviewing:** Whether additional mentoring or role-specific orientation is needed.  
> **When we will review progress:** [date or next cohort].  
> **How to raise a separate concern:** Use the designated confidential safeguarding, privacy, security, or administrative route.

## Facilitator Closing

**Presenter script**

“Thank you for approaching this review with honesty and care. Our next step is to confirm the action register, route any confidential concerns through the correct process, and communicate a short summary to volunteers.

The standard we are aiming for is straightforward: volunteers should know what HMSI stands for, what their role allows them to do, how to get support, how to complete work safely, and how their contribution connects to the mission. When the experience does not meet that standard, our responsibility is to listen, learn, and improve.

Help Meet means we work alongside people. Shine means we help potential and contribution become visible. Initiative means we organize responsible action. Let those three ideas guide how we interpret the feedback and what we do next.”

## Recommended Reporting Language

Use **“respondents reported”** rather than **“volunteers believe”** when the result comes from a subset. Use **“this response set suggests”** rather than **“the programme is failing”** when the evidence is limited. Use **“we are reviewing”** when a finding needs more investigation. Use **“we have referred this through the confidential route”** when a safeguarding, privacy, or security matter is outside ordinary survey analysis.

Avoid publishing raw comments, small-cell breakdowns, individual names, exact contact details, or any information that could expose a volunteer or community member. The purpose of analysis is service improvement with dignity, not surveillance or public ranking.
